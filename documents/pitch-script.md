## Speaker A

### Slide 1: Hook (0:20)

> Imagine the software your company runs on has a critical vulnerability.
> Not hypothetically. This happened last month.
> **Would your team know it was affected, and what to do next?**

### Slide 2: The warning (0:35)

> It's called PHP2Shell. PHP runs a huge share of the web, and in
> October two flaws were disclosed that let an attacker run code on a
> server without logging in. It's rated critical, and national cyber
> agencies told everyone to patch.
>
> But an advisory doesn't answer the two questions that matter to a
> company: **do we run an affected version, and if we do, what's the
> plan?**

*Stay off the technical detail: no CVE numbers, no version ranges.
The score and the source on the slide speak for themselves.*

### Slide 3: Sound familiar? (0:20)

> For most companies, answering those questions looks like this:
> seventeen tabs, three spreadsheets, and one very stressed IT person.
> There has to be a better way.

**Hand-off:**

> A: "[B's name] is going to show you what that looks like."

## Speaker B

### Slide 4: Who it's for (0:30)

> Canary is built for companies that **don't have a large security team
> on the clock around the clock**: startups, clinics, lean IT
> departments. These teams can't afford to drop everything every time a
> critical flaw is announced. But right now, that's exactly what they
> have to do.

### Slide 5: The solution (0:30)

> Canary connects the warning to the response in four steps. It
> **monitors** trusted sources, **matches** what it finds against your
> company's systems, **explains** why it matters and how sure it is, and
> helps you **prepare** a response plan.

### Slide 6: What makes Canary different (0:40)

> Plenty of tools will alert you. Canary does three things they don't.
>
> **One: company context.** Canary knows what you actually run, so an
> alert is about *you*, not just the news.
>
> **Two: a matching rubric.** Every threat gets a score that's fully
> explained, so real emergencies stand out and you're not chasing false
> alarms.
>
> **Three: AI-assisted planning.** Canary drafts a tailored response plan
> in seconds, and your team reviews and approves it.
>
> **Alerting is where most tools stop. It's where Canary starts.** Let me
> show you.

### Slide 8: The business case (0:40)

> So why does this save money? Two reasons.
>
> **First, speed.** The hardest part of a response is starting it. Canary
> drafts the plan the moment a threat matches, so fixes start earlier.
> That shrinks the window attackers have, and **fewer threats turn into
> costly incidents**.
>
> **Second, time.** Today a security lead has to drop everything to build
> a plan from scratch. With Canary, they review a draft instead, and that
> time goes back into **routine checks and audits**: the work that
> prevents the next incident.

### Slide 9: Trust and close (0:25)

> And you can trust it. Every finding links to its source, facts stay
> separate from guesses, and **people make every decision**.
>
> Because "we'll figure it out when it happens" isn't a plan.
> **Canary: early warning, clear action.** Thank you.

## Time-savers

- **Speaker A, if you're running long:** cut the second sentence of
  slide 2 ("PHP runs a huge share…"). The hook still works.
- **Speaker B, if you're running long:** compress slide 5 to one line:
  "Monitor, match, explain, prepare. Here's what makes that different."
- **Never cut:** the three differentiators (slide 6), the two money
  reasons (slide 8), and the closing line.
- **Rehearse with a timer** until each speaker lands within ±10 seconds
  of their total.
